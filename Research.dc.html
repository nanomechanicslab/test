4D Printing of Biopolymers

4D printing is defined as “3D printing + time”, with the fourth dimension being time. 4D printing refers to single-material or multi-material printing of a device or object that can be transformed from a 1D strand into a pre-programmed 3D shape, from a 2D surface into a pre-programmed 3D shape, and is capable of morphing between different dimensions. This advanced technology enables us to fabricate dynamic structures which can change their shapes, functions, or properties with time when appropriate external stimuli (e.g., water, pH, temperature) are applied.

We work with stimuli-responsive smart biopolymers. We perform different experimental characterizations and develop 2D or 3D continuum-scale models to predict the behaviors and the mechanisms involved.

Solvent Triggered Actuation (reversible & irreversible)

Stimuli-responsive biopolymers with advanced functionalities have found a wide range of futuristic applications as soft actuators, morphing medical devices, artificial muscles, soft robotics, sensors, etc. We work with biopolymers (chitosan, PVA etc.) which respond to solvent (water, the simplest one) as an external trigger. The films actuate, or fold and unfold, when exposed to solvent.

The folding is due to differential swelling across the thickness of the film caused by the concentration gradient of solvent. During folding, the polymer chains at the bottom layer experience tension, whereas the top layer chains are in compression. With the passage of time, water diffuses further through the layers of the film, reducing the concentration gradient, thus relaxing the polymer chains to go back to their equilibrium state.

Interestingly, this folding and unfolding behaviour can be controlled by playing with the properties of the polymer matrix and the solvent. For example, in one of our works, we added some organic solvent to the water (used as trigger) and could achieve irreversible or permanent folding. This permanent folding behaviour can be used for gripping and lifting some materials.

Dual Triggered Actuation

It is not always possible or even convenient to have water in bulk around to trigger this folding. In one of our works, we applied a pNIPAM layer as a source of water or solvent supply when the temperature goes beyond its LCST (around 35°C). Figure 1 illustrates the response of the chitosan/pNipam bilayer to temperature. As the lower critical solution temperature of the synthesized pNipam particles is about 32.5°C, the actuation of the film begins around 32.5°C – 35°C. As exemplified in Figure 1A, upon increasing the temperature to 35°C, water released by pNipam goes to the chitosan and triggers the actuator to undergo out-of-plane folding and unfolding. Figure 1B presents the folded state of the actuator, where the pNipam chains become dehydrated and collapsed due to the increase of temperature above the LCST of the microgels. Figure 1C and Figure 1D represent the unfolded state of the chitosan/pNipam bilayer.

Continuum Scale Model

Diffusion of solvent results in a concentration gradient which leads to a displacement field, showing up in the form of folding. During this phenomenon, the diffusion affects the mechanical properties of the polymer matrix, which in turn affects the diffusion — a coupled diffusion–deformation mechanism. We develop a model based on this coupled mechanism which can predict the large deformation caused during the reversible folding behavior of a polymeric thin film. The formulation incorporates a concentration-dependent Young’s modulus variation. This continuum-scale model provides detailed insight into the concentration gradient and strain field developed during folding and unfolding.

Interface Controlled Actuation

The above examples relate to single-layer polymer films. To bring in more diverse applications and apply multiple triggers, it is necessary to apply a bilayer so that the second layer can bring additional functionalities. In one of our works, we introduced PMMA as a second layer on top of the active chitosan layer. When we talk of a bilayer or multilayer, the role of the ‘interface’ is inevitable. We showed, both through modelling and experimentally, that the strength of this interface between PMMA and chitosan can be tuned (strong, weak, weaker, no interface etc.) to achieve different folding behaviour. Continuum-scale models were built to simulate the experimentally obtained interface conditions.

Cement–Polymer–Clay Interface

The application of polymer coating on concrete surfaces is increasing significantly, both as protective coating and/or as aesthetic coating. The applied polymers are of varying functional groups, such as epoxy, PMMA, polyurethane etc. It is usual practice in the construction industry to coat polymers on a concrete surface either when it is completely hard, or when it is old enough. Applying different experimental methods and phase-field modeling techniques, we are trying to determine the most appropriate window for coating a certain polymer on a concrete/cement surface so as to obtain the highest interfacial shear strength.

The continuum-scale FEM model helps us predict crack propagation at the interface and the distribution of stresses around it.

In another project, we are working on developing interfacing between concrete and clay (particularly the smectite group). It is challenging to design a polymer or polymer blend which can interact with the functional groups of both the cement surface and clay surface so as to form a good interface. It is important to determine the right environment of coating, in terms of the instant during hydration of cement and also the clay condition.

Cement Hydration & Microstructure

Needle Based Microstructure

We work on fundamentals of cement hydration, following a multiscale study approach, both experimentally and through simulations. We are primarily interested in the mechanical properties of cement paste in the initial days of hydration. In one of our recent works, we reported that the needle-like interfaces between cement grains play a major role in determining the mechanical properties. SEM images indicate that these needles are unique to the minerals (C3S, C2S, C3A, etc.) and state of hydration (7 days, 14 days, 28 days, etc.). We have combined the microstructures obtained from SEM images, the mechanical properties from nanoindentation and the Finite Element Method to develop a multiscale model capable of predicting mechanical properties of hydrating C3S and C2S. Slowly, we would like to develop a model for cement as a whole.

We have applied our needle-microstructure-based multiscale model to understand the possible mechanism by which Silica Nanoparticles (SNP) modify the mechanical properties of cement.

Molecular Dynamics Simulation of Hydration of Mineral Surfaces

We apply MD to understand the hydrating characteristics of different cement mineral surfaces at the molecular length scale. We have noticed that the behavior (static and dynamic) of the water molecules differs depending on the nature of the surfaces in nearest proximity.

Mechanics of Polymer Thin Films & Nanocomposites

We study the mechanical responses of polymer thin films, their interfaces, etc., applying a combination of experimental techniques and FEM and/or Molecular Dynamics simulations depending on the problem. Both experimentally and through continuum models, we characterize different polymer nanocomposites synthesized in our lab.

Thin Film Interface

Single and multiple layers of polymer thin film (30 to 500 nanometers thick) are found in different engineering, electronic, optical, and data storage applications. Tissue regeneration, implants, stents, and drug delivery are some of the emerging biomedical applications of thin films. In a thin film system with two or more layers of coating, the interface plays an extremely important role in determining the overall behaviour, performance and characteristics of the system. Our group adopts nanomechanical techniques including nanoindentation, nanoscratch and nano-dynamic mechanical analysis (nanoDMA) to study and characterize these interfaces.

Confined Thin Films

One of the major difficulties we encounter while measuring the modulus and hardness of thin films is the ‘substrate effect’. When indenting past a certain depth, the properties of the substrate start influencing the measured film properties significantly, and this substrate effect must be separated from the measured composite values. This ‘substrate effect’, however, can also be used to our advantage. For example, in one of our works, we determined the property of an inaccessible substrate under a changing temperature environment by making use of it. We used experimental data and Finite Element Modeling in the same study to determine the necessary properties (including the glass transition temperature of the substrate).

In another work, applying FEM and nanomechanical experimental techniques, we are trying to determine the role of the different types of water present in the overall mechanical properties of a thin film.

Molecular Mechanics of Solvated Polymer (Molecular Dynamics)

The deformation characteristics of polymer films depend significantly on the response (relaxation, sliding, etc.) of the polymer chains to mechanical loading. Another factor which plays a major role, and is relatively difficult to understand, is the response of the free volume present in the matrix.

For saturated and partially saturated films, the phenomenon is even more complex, since the stress–strain response depends not only on the free volume, but also on how those free volumes are occupied by water and the type of water (free water, bound water etc.). Applying molecular dynamics simulations, we have studied the morphological changes in the free volumes at different strain percentages and, more interestingly, for different degrees of hydration. One of our results indicates that at low strain percentages the free volume mainly undergoes coalescence, whereas at higher strain percentages dissociation of free volume takes place.

Past Research

1. Influence of Mineral Proximity on Mechanics of Protein Domain

Nacre, the inner shiny part of seashells (Fig 1), is composed of 90–95% aragonite (CaCO3) and the rest organics, mainly in the form of proteins and glycoproteins. The aragonite platelets are several microns in dimension in the X and Y directions and only a few nanometers in the Z direction. These platelets are stacked one over another with organics wrapped around them, giving rise to a layered nanocomposite (Fig 2).

Nacre is widely studied as a model biomimetic system for next-generation advanced materials. The fracture toughness of nacre is about 3 orders of magnitude higher than pure aragonite. Previous studies reveal the possible contributions of different microstructural features to this property. The organics present in nacre show a high elastic modulus (15–20 GPa) besides their ability to undergo large deformation before failure. Our studies attempt to understand the possible reasons for this behavior of the organics.

As a part of this study, we focus on understanding the influence of mineral (aragonite) proximity on the mechanical behavior of the organics (protein domain) in nacre.

2. Mechanics of Clay Interlayer with Different Degrees of Hydration

Montmorillonite belongs to the smectite group of clay minerals. It is the most widely studied clay mineral because of its high swelling nature.

The interlayer of sodium montmorillonite was saturated with different water contents and then compressive load was applied. The load–deformation (stress–strain) characteristics were compared for different cases and, most importantly, the interactions of the water molecules with the clay siloxane (silica–oxygen) surface were studied.

Mechanics of Clay Interlayer with Amino Acid as Inclusion Molecules

The sodium ions in montmorillonite were replaced by positively charged amino acids and the load–deformation characteristics of the interlayer were studied under tensile and compressive stress paths. Attempts were also made to understand the interactions of the amino acid molecules with the clay.

3. Modeling and Synthesis of Polymer Clay Nanocomposites (PCN)

Montmorillonite clay is extremely detrimental to civil engineers because of its expansive nature. However, it is the same ability to undergo expansion or exfoliation that makes montmorillonite attractive to materials scientists for the synthesis of polymer clay nanocomposites (PCN). In PCN, polymers (such as nylon 6) are reinforced with clay particles which have one dimension in the nanometer range, hence the name nanocomposite. Clay is usually hydrophilic in nature; it is modified with an organic modifier to make it hydrophobic before it is mixed with the polymer matrix.

The major objectives of this research were to find the interaction between different components of PCN and to understand whether the organic modifiers contribute to the mechanical properties of PCN. Both experimental and modeling techniques were applied.
